# Problem framing


## Domain
Our problem domain is shared informal meals within trusted communities - such as university campuses and workplaces - that extend beyond a person's immediate circle of close friends. Sharing a meal is one of the easiest ways to explore new local restaurants, unwind after classes or work, or celebrate holidays away from family. On campuses, people also occasionally gather at dining halls for quick weeknight meals or subsidized group dinners. While people sometimes choose to eat alone for quiet downtime, there are frequent occasions when someone wants company at a table but their close friends are busy or out of town.

When their immediate circle is unavailable, people turn to broader communities they belong to: dorms, classes, research labs, clubs, or workplace teams. To do this, they communicate through mailing lists, class forums, and existing group chats, as their shared membership gives acquaintances a baseline of trust that makes eating together feel comfortable. Three groups are involved in these meals:
- **Hosts** are community members - students, coworkers, undergraduate academic advisors, or Graduate Resident Advisors (GRAs, live-in dorm mentors who often host meals using dorm budgets or dining-hall swipes) - who want company for a meal or want to help peers connect. They usually have a time window (and often a restaurant or dining hall) in mind, and need firm commitments before booking a table.
- **Prospective guests** are peers in those same channels who want to join a meal to try a new restaurant or avoid eating alone, without having to rally a group from scratch or feel like they are intruding on an existing clique. Unlike close friends, acquaintances do not know each other's dietary restrictions, price sensitivity, or cuisine tastes.
- **Bystanders** are peers in those same channels who aren't interested, whether they are busy or would rather eat alone, yet end up dealing with noisy channels when meal planning devolves into long back-and-forth threads.

Currently, hosts who want to open a meal beyond their close friends send static messages in group chats or mailing lists, or share makeshift spreadsheets and forms. Social event planning tools (such as Partiful) and existing restaurant booking platforms (such as OpenTable) each have their niche: Partiful works well for collecting RSVPs once a host already knows what event they are throwing, while OpenTable handles booking a table once the restaurant and headcount are settled. However, neither helps an acquaintance group move smoothly from "who is free?" to an agreed-upon restaurant and a complete reservation.


## Bad situations

Across both ordinary weeks and holidays, existing channels fail informal meal groups at three recurring points: shy peers stall trying to align unfamiliar diets and budgets, peer hosts lose commitments ahead of booking deadlines without a clean way to reopen seats, and community mentors watch static signup sheets lock out interested residents.

### Bad Situation 1: Shy Holiday-Break Straggler
#### Steps Leading Up:
Noah is an international student from Singapore living in a campus dorm. Flying home for a four-day Thanksgiving break, or any other short holiday, is far too expensive, so he stays behind while his close friends travel home. However, Noah is still part of broader trusted communities, including his dorm mailing list, cultural club chat, and class forums.

#### Moment of Difficulty:
Although Noah's family does not traditionally celebrate Thanksgiving, it feels isolating to know that all his friends are home celebrating with their families while he is stuck on campus; Noah would love to share a Thanksgiving dinner rather than eat alone in the dining hall. Because Noah is shy, posting *"Anyone around for Thanksgiving dinner?"* feels exposing, and scrolling older messages leaves him unsure whether any existing plans still have room. When he finally posts, five acquaintances message him with *"maybe, depends where and how much."* Unlike his close friends, Noah does not know who is vegetarian or halal, what their budgets look like, or who refuses to walk far in the cold. Every restaurant he suggests hits polite pushback over price, diet, or distance, and nobody wants to be the demanding stranger who forces the choice.

#### Consequences:
By the time the back-and-forth drags late into the night, people bail to grab fast food or drift off, leaving Noah eating alone anyway. Additionally, peers who saw his post a few hours late assumed the group had already formed and ate alone rather than risk intruding, a stall that repeats across holidays, long weekends, and summer internships whenever close circles scatter.


### Bad Situation 2: Weeknight Restaurant Explorer Trapped by Soft Replies and Stale Invites
#### Steps Leading Up:
Alice recently started working at a new job and wants to try a neighborhood tapas spot on Thursday night. Her close friends are swamped or live across town, and the restaurant requires a firm headcount by Wednesday evening to hold a table of four to six. She wants to open extra seats to coworkers in a team Slack so she can share dishes, split the bill, and get to know acquaintances she only nods to in passing.

#### Moment of Difficulty:
On Tuesday, Alice drops a casual note: *"Thinking of trying dinner at 7:30 Thursday, anyone want to join?"* Rather than clear commitments she can book against, she fields scattered questions - *"How pricey?"*, *"Still room?"*, *"Can I bring a friend?"* - and ends up repeating the same answers across side messages while cluttering the channel for busy bystanders. By Tuesday night, four peers have replied *"sounds good!"* or *"maybe"*, so Alice assumes the table is full and tells a fifth interested coworker there are no more spots. On Wednesday afternoon, one of the four realizes they have a conflict and backs out over text. Because the original invite is buried in Slack history, Alice cannot gracefully let the channel know that seats have reopened without sending another noisy blast.

#### Consequences:
By Wednesday evening's booking deadline, Alice is stuck either locking in a smaller table - leaving out the fifth coworker she already turned away and other quiet peers who never knew seats reopened - or scrambling through DMs to find replacements. After repeating this herding effort a few times on ordinary weeknights, hosts stop opening tables to wider circles at all.


### Bad Situation 3: GRA and Advisor Meal-Swipe Spreadsheets Going Stale
#### Steps Leading Up:
Maya is a Graduate Resident Advisor (GRA) for a student dorm floor. She receives a recurring budget and guest meal swipes meant to help students step away from coursework and bond over informal meals. Every weekend, Maya wants to take ten residents to a campus dining hall or nearby casual spot without turning it into a stiff, formal event.

#### Moment of Difficulty:
To cap group size, Maya emails the floor a shared Google Sheet with ten signup rows. Within hours, the sheet breaks down in two ways. First, when slots fill quickly, the sheet looks finalized to anyone who checks email later; even if a student later removes their name because of a midterm conflict, past viewers have no reason to reopen the spreadsheet and never learn a spot opened unless Maya emails the whole floor again. Second, when slots fill slowly, residents cannot tell whether the meal is still happening, so hesitant residents skip signing up.

#### Consequences:
Maya arrives at the dining hall with empty spots that could have gone to interested residents on the same floor who saw a full sheet earlier and ate alone instead. Over the semester, GRAs and advisors tire of chasing spreadsheets and answering one-off emails, and cut back on hosting informal weekend meals, shrinking one of the easiest ways for busy students to meet peers outside their classes.


## Corroboration

This section supports three claims that the rest of the framing depends on, namely that a list of interested people is not yet a seat, that many students hesitate before sending the first message, and that some students want company over a break while others would rather have quiet.

### A list of interested people is not yet a seat

The local evidence comes from a classmate's account of the Maseeh-night sheet, where a fixed number of slots filled up with names and students who saw the email late were closed out. Another classmate who has spent Thanksgiving on campus also recognized the situation in our pitch. Both are single accounts, so they show that the situation is familiar at MIT but not how often it happens.

Yale's [Thanksgiving at Home](https://oiss.yale.edu/programs-events/thanksgiving-at-home-nov-2026) and Buffalo's [Thanksgiving without Borders](https://www.buffalo.edu/international-student-services/workshops-trips-events/thanksgiving-without-borders.html) are the public version of the host's count. Thanksgiving at Home tells students that a place depends on a host's confirmation, and it asks them to keep the day free because a late cancellation falls on a family that has already planned the meal. Thanksgiving without Borders similarly says that registration does not guarantee placement, and that a last-minute cancellation wastes the host's time and money. These offices already treat a casual yes as too weak to cook from, which is the same judgment behind our seats. However, they do not show that software would create more hosts, and each one covers a single holiday rather than a GRA dinner in March or a game night on a long weekend. How these programs compare to our approach is covered under workarounds below.

### Hesitation before the first message

Two studies help explain why Noah hesitates to post, although neither one says anything about the spreadsheet. Epley and Schroeder found that commuters told to talk with a nearby stranger reported a better ride than commuters told to sit alone, while a separate group predicted the opposite, in part because they underestimated how willing the stranger would be to talk ([Mistakenly Seeking Solitude, 2014](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/jschroeder/Publications/Epley%26Schroeder2014.pdf)). A commuter train is different from a potluck, so the relevant piece is only the hesitation before the first message.

Chandler and colleagues surveyed 255 students at one UK university and found higher loneliness among those who ate alone because they were uneasy reaching out, uncomfortable eating with others, or had nobody to eat with ([Eating Together, Eating Alone, 2026 preprint](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202603.2194/v1)). About 16% of the students surveyed scored in the severe range on the ULS-8. The study is cross-sectional, so it cannot show whether eating alone caused the loneliness, and it was run on a different campus.

### Some students want company, and some want quiet

Forum posts are weaker evidence, and they point in two directions. In a [Berkeley thread](https://www.reddit.com/r/berkeley/comments/z28cx7/what_to_do_on_thanksgiving/), a student whose family lived too far away to visit for Thanksgiving, and whose roommates and friends were all leaving, asked what people usually do over the holiday. The top reply came from someone who had spent Thanksgiving on campus years earlier, who recommended enjoying the campus while it is empty and quiet, and also suggested that the student later "collect the strays," since there are always people in that situation who would appreciate an invitation to a holiday meal. In a [Maryland thread](https://www.reddit.com/r/UMD/comments/pin4f4/since_dining_halls_wont_be_open_during/), an international student with nowhere to go during breaks learned that they could stay in the residence halls while the dining halls closed, and asked what alternatives other students had. The replies offered a joke and a grocery store within walking distance, but nothing about where to find a meal with other people, which is Noah's problem in a different form. These are anonymous posts from other campuses, and people who had a hard week are more likely to write them.

Interviews with 13 students at an English university about meals before the 2020 lockdown split along the same line ([Eating alone or together, Food and Foodways, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/07409710.2024.2333119)). Some students wanted the solo meal as a break, while others avoided the canteen so they would not be seen sitting alone. Together, these sources support a distinction that the design keeps. The gatherings in this project are for students who want company, and students who want the quiet should not have to do anything. An invitation can matter to a student who is avoiding being seen alone, but it should never be imposed on a student who chose to eat alone.

## Workarounds and comparables

Students currently combine a few tools to handle two separate jobs, which are inviting people and managing a headcount. The table below summarizes what each tool does well, where it breaks, and which of our bad situations (BS) it fails in, and the paragraphs after it explain the less obvious entries.

| Workaround | What it does well | Where it breaks | Bad situations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Group chats, mailing lists, lab Slacks | Reaches people the host already shares a channel with | A "maybe" does not hold a place, invites get buried, and a freed place cannot be announced without another blast | BS1, BS2 |
| Class forums (Piazza, Commons) | Wider reach within one class | Same problems as chats, plus noise for bystanders | BS1 |
| Google Sheets and Forms | A cap, a list of names, and one link | Late viewers see "full", and removed names are never noticed by anyone who already checked | BS2, BS3 |
| Partiful, Facebook Events | RSVPs once the host knows whom to invite | Heavy for one open chair, and a freed place does not reach people outside the invite | BS2 |
| OpenTable | Booking once the venue and headcount are settled | Does nothing before that point | BS2 |
| University hosting programs (Yale, Buffalo) | Staff, a warning that the meal is a real commitment, and host matching | One holiday only, and not every student who signs up is placed | BS1 |
| Walking up to a table or lounge | Works when it is obvious someone wants company | The approach itself is the hard part for some students | BS1 |

Group chats are what Noah already has. They reach the people in the chat, and over a break many of those people have left. A message asking for company can go unanswered even when someone nearby would have come. A reply of "I might come" also does not hold a place, and the silence is visible to the rest of the chat, which is part of why the next ask does not get sent.

A spreadsheet gives the host a cap, a list of names, and one link. Maseeh nights ran on that. Students still have to see the email before the cells fill, and a student who changes plans has to remember to delete a name. If they do not, the slot stays occupied. If a home address is in the same email, forwarding the invitation forwards the address. The sheet is good at collecting a list from people the host already knows how to email. It is poor at returning a freed slot to someone who was never on that email.

Yale's and Buffalo's offices add staff, a warning that the meal is a real commitment, and a match between students and hosts, which makes them the serious version of holiday hospitality. As noted under corroboration, they still cannot place every student who signs up, and they do not cover a GRA dinner or a lounge game night outside of that one holiday.

Walking over to a table or a lounge works when it is obvious that someone wants another person there. The English interviews suggest that some students skip shared dining rooms because they do not want to be seen alone. For them, the approach itself is the hard part. Knowing in advance that a particular gathering has a place for them is a different action from crossing a room.

Partiful and Facebook Events collect invitations and RSVPs, which is the right job when a host has already decided whom to invite and is willing to run an event page. However, they are a heavy tool for "a few of us are in the lounge, and there is a fourth chair." Services that assign dinner companions among strangers start from the opposite end, because the other people are the product and the host is not someone who was already going to be there. Our case starts with that host, and with a community the guest already belongs to.

None of these returns a released place to other people in the community who never got the original message, and none of them lets news of a home gathering circulate while the street address stays with the people who have claimed a place. Several critiques suggested profiles so a guest could judge whether they would get along with a host. A profile might answer that question. It would not tell Noah that a Maseeh slot had opened, or that he was allowed to take it.

## Solution sketch

Our solution is a **link-first shared meal organizer** built for trusted communities such as university campuses and workplaces. Rather than forcing people to abandon the mailing lists, Slacks, and group chats they already check, a host creates a lightweight **table page** and drops its shareable link directly into those existing channels (much like sharing a Zoom link).

1. **Public details without DM clutter (addresses BS1, BS2, and BS3):** Anyone who opens the link immediately sees the meal's time window, seat capacity, how many spots remain, budget tier, dietary notes (such as vegetarian or halal options), and the RSVP cutoff - whether it is a neighborhood tapas spot, a restaurant shortlist, or a GRA dining-hall trip. Shy peers like Noah never have to send an awkward *"is there still room?"* message, and hosts like Alice and Maya do not have to repeat the same details across private messages or emails.

2. **Two-option system for choosing a restaurant (addresses BS1 and BS2):**
   - **Host selects a restaurant (no poll):** When a host already has a restaurant or dining hall in mind (as in Alice's tapas outing or Maya's floor dinner), no poll is created—guests simply claim an open seat.
   - **Host leaves the restaurant unselected (invitees vote):** When an organizer has a time window but wants the group to choose together without hitting acquaintance deadlock (as in Noah's break dinner), the host sets a time, seat cap, and optional initial restaurant suggestions without picking a final venue. Users can save a lightweight **dining preference profile** (dietary restrictions, budget preference, cuisine tastes, and walking preference) so participants at the table can see each other's constraints without putting anyone on the spot, and committed invitees propose and vote on options before the cutoff to lock in one restaurant. Importantly, we avoid personality or social compatibility matching as well as automated restaurant recommendations; profiles exist purely to inform human venue choices.

3. **Verified community access without manual approval (addresses BS1 and BS2):** The host selects one or more communities that can join (for example, anyone with an `@mit.edu` or company email, or a specific dorm or group, so that one table can be open to both a dorm and a second community at once). Even if the link is forwarded, claiming a seat requires verifying membership in that community. Within those bounds, eligible peers claim an open spot directly without waiting for host approval, avoiding both host bottlenecking and the social discomfort of asking to be accepted.

4. **Live seat tracking, cutoffs, and automatic reopening (addresses BS2 and BS3):** Because every guest holds an explicit seat rather than a soft *"maybe"*, the host gets an exact headcount by the booking deadline. Holding a seat triggers a timely reminder ahead of the cutoff; if a diner's plans change and they release their spot before then, the seat automatically becomes open again on the exact same link and notifies anyone on a waitlist. A host with a fixed venue can place the actual reservation (for example, on OpenTable or by phone) and mark the table booked right away or once the headcount settles, while a host whose group is voting books once the restaurant and headcount lock in at the cutoff.

5. **Community table board alongside shareable links (addresses BS1):** Inside each verified community, an optional day-by-day list displays open tables with remaining seats - answering the basic question *"what meals have room tonight?"* without requiring a hesitant newcomer to post into a silent hundred-person channel first.

**Scope and realistic limits:** The system cannot force someone to show up after a booking deadline passes, nor can it guarantee that a meal turns into a close friendship. It does, however, remove the friction between *"anyone free?"* and a confirmed reservation: dead spreadsheet rows and dropped seats reopen automatically, acquaintances align on compatible restaurants without awkward negotiation, and people who prefer eating alone never have to interact with the tool. We plan to pilot first within MIT communities (across both ordinary weeks and academic breaks) before expanding to other campuses and workplaces.

## Ethical analysis

The analysis below uses the four lecture dimensions required by F2. Each entry names its dimension, describes the naive implementation (what a first version of the feature would do) and the harm that follows from it, and then gives the design response we chose and the risk that remains. Multi-lifespan cards are outside the scope of this assignment. In this section, a *table page* is the shareable page for one gathering, and the *table board* is the day-by-day list of open tables inside a community.

### 1. Indirect stakeholders: home addresses
- **Naive implementation:** The table page shows the street address so that guests know where to go.
- **Harm:** A potluck at a professor's apartment also involves roommates and neighbors, who have no account and no way to opt out, and the address then follows every forward of the link.
- **Design response:** The table page only shows a general area, and the full address is visible only to the host and to whoever currently holds a place. Dining halls and lounges can still be listed in full.
- **Remaining risk:** A guest who has seen the address can still pass it on later.

### 2. Values: community and dignity in who gets a place
- **Naive implementation:** The host approves each guest, or guests fill out profiles that the host browses before deciding.
- **Harm:** Approval protects an evening, but it makes a spare seat depend on whether a stranger seems pleasant, and profiles sort people before they meet.
- **Design response:** The host sets the community, the capacity, and terms such as food or a contribution, and anyone inside those terms can claim an open place without being ranked. Dining preferences are only used to narrow down where the group can eat.
- **Remaining risk:** Hosts can no longer turn a particular person away. Guests and reluctant hosts both avoid that rejection, but a host also cannot screen guests for an evening they want to keep small.

### 3. Time: non-use and the invitation link
- **Naive implementation:** Gatherings exist only inside the app.
- **Harm:** Students who never install the app miss open places the same way they missed the Maseeh email.
- **Design response:** Each table page has a link that the host can send in the old chat or mailing list, while claiming a seat still checks membership in the selected community. Reminders only go to people holding a place, which means that students who want a quiet break are never prompted to attend.
- **Remaining risk:** A table that nobody shares only reaches the members who open the table board.

### 4. Pervasiveness: what "bring something" comes to mean
- **Naive implementation:** Table pages prompt guests to bring food by default.
- **Harm:** If most tables ask for food, students who stayed on campus because travel was already expensive are shut out, and an optional dish starts to sound required.
- **Design response:** By default nothing is required, and a host has to mark a contribution as optional or expected. A dietary or religious note on the same page helps both a guest who cannot eat the meal and a host who would otherwise have to guess.
- **Remaining risk:** A host can still mark a contribution as expected, so the default only protects tables where the host leaves it alone.

### 5. Values: community and safety at private homes
- **Naive implementation:** An open table board shows every gathering to everyone.
- **Harm:** The board would show Noah more gatherings, but it would also let someone outside the community claim a place at an apartment, and some first-years are under 18.
- **Design response:** Claims stay inside the community the host selects, and a home address stays off the table page. We still allow home gatherings, because hosts already invite people to them over email, where the address circulates with no check at all.
- **Remaining risk:** Shared affiliation is not the same as safety, so membership is a weak safeguard and should not be presented as more than that.

### 6. Time: reappropriation, once a place means something else
- **Naive implementation:** The app supports any kind of event, with a follower list for popular hosts.
- **Harm:** If open places get used for dates or large parties, a student looking for a small meal can no longer trust the table board.
- **Design response:** Table types stay limited to meals, potlucks, and game nights, and each table closes when its window ends. There is no follower list, and a released place returns as a seat on the same link.
- **Remaining risk:** Some misuse will remain, since the board can only show that a gathering has room and cannot tell a guest what the gathering will be like.

### 7. Direct stakeholders: fake, abandoned, and spam tables
- **Naive implementation:** Anyone can post any number of tables, and a table stays listed until someone closes it.
- **Harm:** Guests may show up to a table that does not exist, while spam fills the board and wastes the attention of bystanders, who are already the group most exposed to noise.
- **Design response:** Only verified community members can create tables, so every post is tied to an institutional identity. The host also has to confirm the table before its cutoff, and a table that is not confirmed closes and drops off the board. Each host can only have a limited number of tables open at once, and attendees can report a table, which sends it to a short review queue.
- **Remaining risk:** A verified member can still post one false table before anyone notices, and the review queue depends on people we may not have by the beta. As a result, the development plan treats the report queue as optional and builds host confirmation and the per-host limit first.
